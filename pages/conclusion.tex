\section{Conclusion}
Among the years as technology kept evolving so did
virtualisation. It evolved from a theoretical concept
defined by IBM’s project and academic research into a
critical technology that supports multiple operating
systems through various architecture approaches. While
the early methods like trap and emulate were once
considered the only viable techniques, as shown modern
strategies have developed to include operating system
virtualisation and hardware assisted methods that make
use of extensions of the Central Processing Unit for an
increase in performance.
The distinction between type I and type II hypervisors
serves as a highlight the different ways in which resource
management, for example CPU scheduling, can be
handled either directly above the hardware or within the
host OS. A significant part of the virtualization burden
relies on memory management. Hypervisors must
maintain its isolation and security by enforcing additional
layers of address translation. While para-virtualization is
offering reduced complexity by allowing guests to
interface directly with the hypervisor, the execution of
unmodified guest OS’s require full virtualization of x86
paging, which result in significant overhead. Ultimately,
the choice of virtualisation relies on balancing the trade
offs between performance overhead such as that caused
by binary translation and the flexibility of running
unmodified legacy OS’s.
